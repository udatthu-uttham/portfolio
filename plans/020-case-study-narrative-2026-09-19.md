# How leaders tell case studies — research and a model for Mall (2026-09-19)

**Status: for discussion. Nothing beyond the plans/019 draft is built.**

## 1. What the references do

| Source | What it argues | What it means for Mall |
|---|---|---|
| **Helena Seo, Head of Design at DoorDash** (hired 30+ managers) | A leader's case study is *storytelling, not a status report*: a clear problem, "the proper fidelity of the sausage-making", a moment of revelation, learnings and next steps, and an unexpected twist. Balance team-ops with product innovation, vision with execution, business impact with user need, breadth with depth, and *leading from the front* with servant leadership. Say what wouldn't have happened without you. Simplify: a friend outside the industry must grasp the problem. | Mall has the twist built in (2.9 % and an award, then flat). Lead with the problem in plain words: "branded" meant nothing to shoppers who had never met a brand online. The pivot from seller labels to real brands is the *what wouldn't have happened without you*. |
| **Verified Insider, hiring-manager survey** | Leadership hiring managers are often not designers. They want "live work, some data, and options to see more of the process later." Visual and outcome-first; process on demand. Show team quality and the environment you built, not just your hands. | Outcome and one image first; process folded away; the team's part visible. |
| **Mitchell Clements** (the exemplar named by the survey) | Executive summary in five lines (role, timeframe, company size, model). Then a *linear story* in short chapters with punchy kickers ("A very bold move", "Ideas are easy. Strategy is the hard part."), one image per chapter, one stat block near the end, quotes from others, a reflection, and a hand-off to the next study. Detail is kept out by cutting, not by hiding. The page is long but each chapter is short. | Our five-beat spine already matches. Add a five-line summary strip at the top and a hand-off at the end (we have the "next case" control). Kickers become our eyebrows. |
| **Alex Couch, "9 micro case studies"** | Real work isn't storybook-linear. Each micro study is *Problem / Outcome* in two lines, then two paragraphs, with a link to the full study for those who want it. Progressive disclosure is the tool. | Every chapter opens with a one-line problem and a one-line outcome that can be read alone; the paragraph is optional depth. |
| **UXfolio, eight tips** | Personality and your specific role; *why* a method was chosen; skimmability; only polished visuals, omit rather than show weak ones; reflection and next steps; credit collaborators in context. | The deck's mockups need re-cropping before they go on glass; the People slide becomes credit-in-context, not a section about management. |

**What none of them do, and we can:** our materials already encode disclosure. Paper is the story; glass is the evidence; handwriting is the aside. A reader who wants only the story never has to look at the glass.

## 2. The model — a story with the working folded in

**Read in ninety seconds.** Hero (title, one-line summary, five-line fact strip: role, team, years, scale, ask). Five eyebrows + headings read top to bottom as the whole story: *The bet · Bold wasn't enough · The turn · Colour as trust · What it did.* Three plain numbers. One verbatim on a sticky note. One line on what's next. The next-case control.

**Read in five minutes.** Each chapter's paragraph (60–90 words, present in the draft) and its glass figure with a handwritten caption.

**Read in fifteen, if you want the working.** Each chapter carries a folded **"Show the working"** note — a paper tab that opens in place to reveal the layer the deck has and the page hides: the journey map, the problem/objective pairs, the research method (diary studies, interviews, the logo readability test), the v3 screen-by-screen list, the v4 roadmap. Closed by default; a reader who never opens one loses nothing. This is the deck's detail, available only when asked for, and it replaces the receipts we removed from "How I lead" with something that has a reason to exist.

**Leadership, shown not claimed.** No "People" chapter. Instead, the leadership beat's substance goes into the story where it happened: the audit-style advocacy against seller labels (Seo's "leading from the front"), the ground-level research in homes, the team owning the pivot. One short closing chapter, "How I led it", stays as the reflection Clements and Seo both end with, two or three sentences, credit in context.

**Visuals, from the deck, tweaked.** Keep the deck's idea of annotated phone screens, change the execution: phones cropped tight on the glass artboard, two or three per figure, never a wall; the deck's purple callout arrows become handwritten Caveat notes with a thin ink leader; the amber highlighter marks the one thing to look at in each screen. The v3 figure can be a small before/after (old pill / new tick) since that *is* the story. Purple stays inside the screenshots.

**Voice.** Kickers in the site's clipped register ("Bold wasn't enough"). Paragraphs in first person, past tense, no jargon a non-designer can't follow (NMV gets a one-word gloss the first time). One twist per chapter, stated plainly.

## 3. What I need from Uttham to make it great

1. **The shopper.** Who was the Mall buyer in 2021, in one or two lines a stranger would understand: where they live, what they buy, why "branded" was unfamiliar. The deck quotes the user base only in aggregate and never describes a person.
2. **The stakes.** Why did the business care about brands — margin, trust, competition? One sentence makes the bet legible.
3. **The seller-labels fight.** Who wanted them, what you said, how the decision turned. This is the leading-from-the-front moment and the deck only hints at it.
4. **The research, concretely.** How many diary studies and interviews, in which cities, over how long. The Lucknow verbatim suggests home visits; say so.
5. **Your team.** Names or roles of the designers on Mall, and one thing one of them did that the story should credit.
6. **The quiet failure.** Anything that didn't work in v3 (Seo's "sausage-making fidelity"; Couch's honesty). One admitted miss makes the rest believable.
7. **Numbers you're comfortable with on the page**, later; the deck's stay for the draft.
8. **Figma access** for the screens, as agreed.

## 4. Not doing
Stat tiles, hero video, testimonial carousel, a "process" section, a methods list, a persona card, any purple outside screenshots.

motion that tells one beat and rests. Deck slide → site piece:

   Confirmation; a dot travels on scroll; each node opens its real screen on glass on hover/tap. Disclosure

6. **How I led it**: no new drawing.
